# Worked example: structure and revision for an original short story

Illustrative scenario, **not executed** as a writer trial, reader study, or recorded model trial. Two authored craft essays were inspected on **2026-10-02**. The brief, hypothetical drafts, comparison, and process below are original demonstration material, not a completed or tested story. They establish neither reader response nor the suite's creative effectiveness.

## Constraints

A hypothetical writer is making a 1,200-1,500-word story for a print zine. Two neighbors sort abandoned objects in an apartment building's laundry room after a flood. One neighbor believes the other discarded a family possession. The story should move from accusation toward an uneasy willingness to help, without declaring complete reconciliation. It has one narrator, one location, no supernatural explanation, and a weekend deadline. It must not borrow a recognizable plot, signature imagery, or another writer's voice.

The creative goal is specific: readers should infer a change through actions rather than receive an explanation of what to feel. This is not a task to choose a writing app or automate prose generation. The decision concerns how to plan and revise the work while preserving that intent.

## Search scope

A live task would inspect the writer's draft and notes first. None is supplied here, so no supposed diagnosis of their writing is reported. Source inspection was limited to Edgar Allan Poe's *The Philosophy of Composition* in a transcription identifying its April 1846 publication, and George Saunders's authored 2017 essay on his writing process. These were chosen to compare two relevant methods directly, not to survey all story structures.

No audience interviews, zine editorial requirements beyond the hypothetical brief, other craft traditions, or completed stories were assessed. Neither essay is controlled research. Poe discusses composing a poem; Saunders discusses revision and his novel. Applying either to this short story is an explicit inference.

## Examined options

1. Plan backward from an ending and intended effect. Poe argues for keeping the ending in view and choosing incidents and tone toward an effect. Applied here, define the final action before drafting: one neighbor leaves space on the drying rack for the other's damaged objects. Earlier accusations and gestures would need to make that change credible. This can focus a short piece, but it can also make every action serve a predetermined lesson.
2. Discover the story through repeated revision. Saunders describes small preference-based adjustments and pursuit of specificity, with the piece changing beyond the writer's original intention. Applied here, draft the interaction, then replace abstract judgments with behavior and revise whatever changes the reader's understanding. It allows discovery, but the long iterative process he describes is not evidence that the same approach fits a weekend deadline.
3. Copy an admired work's beat sequence or voice. This supplies a ready-made shape but risks replacing the brief's emotional and material choices with the reference's. It is not necessary to learn from either essay. Reject this option, and route any deliberate adaptation or reuse to a rights check.
4. Compose a small ending constraint with a bounded discovery pass. Choose a tentative final action, draft toward it, and allow revision to change its meaning or remove it. This combines useful methods without accepting either as a complete specification.

## Lessons and failure modes

Poe's account connects extent, unity of effect, tone, and an ending. Its lesson here is that a length constraint affects structure: several unrelated subplots would compete with the brief's central interaction. His account is an author's published explanation, not independently verified evidence of every step he took. It does not prove that planning backward is mandatory or that one intended effect governs all good fiction.

Saunders's discussion of revision moves from broad labels toward specific action and context; he also questions the idea that art simply executes a clear initial intention. The lesson is to inspect whether a sentence provides evidence for the judgment it asks a reader to accept. His experience does not establish a universal number of drafts or a predictable improvement rate.

For this brief, a likely planning failure is resolving the argument too neatly because the outline demanded reconciliation. A likely discovery failure is expanding the history of every object until the story no longer fits its form. Both are inferred risks, not reported test results. The brief's unresolved tension helps reject the first; a limited revision scope helps contain the second.

Consider this invented diagnostic pair, not a tested rewrite:

- Draft A: "Mara understood that her neighbor was kind, forgave him, and felt hopeful."
- Draft B: "Mara moved her father's photograph away from the heater. Tomas held out a dry towel. She took one corner and left the other in his hand."

Draft B supplies an action a reader could interpret and leaves the relationship unsettled. It might still feel staged or imply too much forgiveness; only the completed scene and reader feedback could resolve that. The contrast illustrates what to inspect, not a measured preference between drafts.

## Comparison

| Approach | Fits the brief because | Main risk | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full ending-first outline | Focuses causal movement in one short encounter | Characters become devices for a preset conclusion | Borrow the ending constraint, not a rigid full outline |
| Open-ended discovery | Allows unexpected gestures and motives | Revision expands beyond the deadline and length | Borrow a focused revision pass, not an unlimited process |
| Model-work imitation | Provides a ready-made sequence | Imports someone else's treatment instead of this intent | Reject |
| Tentative ending + revision | Retains focus while allowing evidence to change the plan | The tentative ending may still bias the draft | Provisional compose decision, with a test for that bias |

## Bounded decision

Compose the two methods for this draft only. Write a one-sentence intended effect and a tentative final gesture. Sketch three moments: accusation, a concrete contradiction to the narrator's assumption, and shared practical action without an explicit declaration of forgiveness. These are proposed beats for this scenario, not a universal story template.

Draft once. Make a focused pass replacing unsupported emotional conclusions with specific actions where that serves the intended effect. Then test the plan's bias: would the ending still make sense if the narrator remained partly angry? If every sentence has to suppress that anger, change the ending rather than forcing the character to comply.

The next unexecuted probe is to give the completed draft to readers without explaining its intended effect and ask what they think changed between the neighbors, which actions support that reading, and whether anything felt forced. Disagreement might suggest useful ambiguity or missing evidence; it is not automatically failure. A second pass should address the brief's specific mismatches, not imitate the essays' styles. If the original user requested a story, hand back from research and write that authorized artifact; this comparison alone is not the story.
