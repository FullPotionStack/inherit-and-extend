# Games

Load for an engine, mechanic, production approach, or game-format decision.

## Sources

Inspect local prototypes, design notes, assets, and tool constraints first. Use engine documentation/source for capabilities and licenses. Find practitioner accounts through [GDC Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/), [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/), [itch.io](https://itch.io/), engine forums, and the developer's own devlog or postmortem. Search for the mechanic and its player-facing purpose, not only a famous title that used it.

A paid talk you cannot access is a lead. A trailer establishes what was shown, not its implementation or development cost.

## Compare

Check platform, team experience, genre/mechanic, asset pipeline, accessibility, controls, save behavior, production deadline, and intended player experience. Separate an engine feature from whether it supports that experience at the required scale. A shipped studio's solution may depend on staffing, custom tooling, or content volume absent from a solo project.

Look for cut features, playtest failures, scope overruns, or pipeline problems. Attribute lessons to their context rather than treating successful games as representative evidence. If a devlog reports that players missed a cue, ask whether the cue, audience, and playtest format resemble yours.

## Decision and probe

Adopt a suitable engine or established technique, extend a prototype, compose compatible mechanics, or build a narrow custom component. Recommend one testable slice: the actual input-to-feedback loop, a save/load boundary, or an accessible interaction. Mark unrun playtests as pending; do not infer fun, usability, or commercial success from technical feasibility.

Learning a pacing principle or interaction pattern is distinct from copying levels, dialogue, art, audio, or code. Check rights before actual reuse. Keep the user's intended experience visible when comparing precedent.
